Average Axial Growth Rate by Age — the Reference Numbers

How fast is a child's eye supposed to grow? These are the working reference values behind the 6S tracker's warning logic.

Eyes that stay non-myopic still grow — just slowly. Pooled studies of children who remained emmetropic show physiological elongation falling from about 0.17 mm/year at age 6 to about 0.03 mm/year by age 16, essentially stopping in the mid-teens. A child whose eyes grow faster than the age band below is very likely on the road to myopia — and that bend in the curve is visible years before blur.

Physiological values are rounded from pooled emmetropic-eye models of large multi-cohort datasets (East Asian and European children; boys' eyes run slightly longer and grow about 7% faster). The "good target" and "fast-growth flag" columns are clinical working rules, not diagnosis thresholds — always interpret with your eye-care professional.

How to use this table

1

Never grade a single measurement

A 7-year-old at 23.2 mm can be perfectly normal (tall kid, flat cornea). Compute the rate: (today − last time) ÷ years between. Two measurements at the same clinic, same device, at least 3 months apart.

2

Compare the rate to the age band

Below the physiological band: on track. Persistently above it: the eye is outpacing childhood. For a child already on myopia-control treatment, the "good target" column is what success looks like — slowing to near-physiological speed is a win, even if growth doesn't reach zero.

3

Expect the deceleration

Growth naturally slows with age — a flat curve at 15 means something different than a flat curve at 8. Judge a treatment only against what the eye would have done at that age. This is why the tracker anchors your child's curve to age-based percentiles instead of a single "normal" line.

Where these numbers come from

The physiological bands synthesize published models of emmetropic eye growth — including the classic longitudinal datasets (e.g., Zadnik and colleagues, where non-myopic children's eyes grew from about 22.6 to 23.3 mm between ages 6 and 14 while staying emmetropic) and recent large pooled analyses spanning tens of thousands of children, which found growth declining non-linearly from ~0.17 to ~0.03 mm/year between ages 6 and 16, with small sex and ethnic differences. This page is an educational reference, continuously updated as new normative data publishes.

Educational reference only. Individual eyes vary with height, corneal shape and ethnicity. These bands inform conversations with your eye-care professional; they do not replace one.

Where does your child's lane end at 18?

One measurement is enough to start. Enter your child's age and latest axial length, pick the reference population that matches your child — the CREAM consortium's European/Australian database (sex-specific curves, ages 6–18) or the Chinese reference (sex-combined, ages 3–18) — and we pinpoint their exact percentile lane, project it to age 18 (axial length and estimated refraction), and grade the risk. Children who hold their lane tend to land where the lane ends; children who climb lanes are the ones to act on early.

Method note: the projection assumes the eye holds its current percentile lane to age 18 — a simplification used for orientation, not a clinical prediction. The refraction estimate applies the rule of thumb of −2.5 to −3.0 D per mm beyond a 24 mm emmetropic baseline; actual refraction depends on corneal and lens power, which axial length alone cannot see; growth spurts and interventions also move the outcome. Axial length around 26 mm and beyond is the range where high-myopia complications climb steeply. Educational tool, not medical advice.
