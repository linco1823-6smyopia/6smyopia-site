Your Child Just Failed a Vision Screening. The First 24 Hours.

The school note says "failed vision screening — please see an eye doctor." Your stomach drops. Breathe. Here is exactly what to do, in order.

A failed screening is not a diagnosis — it is a smoke alarm, not a fire. Screenings catch real problems early, but they also produce false alarms. Three things to do in the next 24 hours: save the notice, book a proper exam, start outdoor time today. And three things to never do: don't buy glasses on the spot, don't buy miracle cures, don't panic in front of your child.

Three things to do — today

1

Save the screening notice (photo it right now)

That slip of paper is the first data point of your child's record. Photograph it, note the date, and keep any numbers it shows. When you later track axial length, this day becomes "day zero" of the timeline.

2

Book a real exam — with two specific requests

Call an optometrist or ophthalmologist and ask for: (a) cycloplegic refraction (drops that relax focusing — children's focusing muscles can fake extra myopia on a quick test) and (b) an axial-length measurement (a 10-second, no-touch scan). These two numbers, not the screening, decide what happens next. If the exam comes back normal, you lost nothing but a co-pay — and gained a baseline.

3

Start the free protection today

Whatever the exam finds: two hours outdoors daily is the single best-evidenced way to delay myopia onset, and it starts working before any prescription exists. Add the 30-20-10 rule for homework sessions. No purchase required.

THE 30-20-10 RULE

Every 30 minutes of close work → look at something 20 feet (6 m) away → for at least 10 seconds. Longer breaks are better; this is the floor, not the ceiling.

Three things to never do

  • Don't buy glasses the same day at the mall. A screening-grade result without cycloplegic drops can overstate myopia. The prescription deserves the full exam.
  • Don't buy "vision restoration" courses, pinhole glasses, or miracle devices. No product reverses eye growth; anyone promising a cure is selling to your panic, not your child's eyes.
  • Don't frame it as the child's fault. Myopia is driven by modern indoor life and genetics, not by "reading wrong." The child who feels blamed hides squinting. The child who feels recruited becomes your teammate.

If the exam confirms myopia

Still not a catastrophe — it is the start of management, and management works. Read what to do when the tracker shows a warning, get the axial-length baseline, and remember the goal: not to avoid glasses, but to keep a 7-year-old's small prescription from becoming a 17-year-old's −6.00 D. Every diopter you don't gain is risk you permanently removed.
