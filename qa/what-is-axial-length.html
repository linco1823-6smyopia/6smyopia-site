What Is Axial Length — and Why Does It Matter More Than the Eye Chart?

If you only learn one number about your child's eyes, make it this one.

Axial length is the front-to-back length of the eyeball, measured in millimeters. Myopia is, at its core, an eye that has grown too long — every extra millimeter is roughly 2.5–3.0 diopters of nearsightedness. The eye chart tells you how blurry today is; the axial length tells you where your child is heading — and it can warn you before the prescription changes.

What the number actually measures

Axial length is the distance from the front surface of the cornea to the retina at the back of the eye. It is measured with a painless, non-contact light scan (an optical biometer — common models are the IOLMaster and Lenstar) that takes a few seconds per eye. No drops, no touching, no guesswork.

A typical adult eye is about 24 mm long. A child who is becoming myopic is not "losing vision" — the eye is physically stretching. Glasses refocus the image, but they do not stop the stretch. That is why the 6S Method tracks the length itself: the prescription is the symptom; the axial length is the disease.

Why it beats the eye chart

Three reasons:

  • It leads. Eyes typically elongate for months before the blur shows up on a chart or a prescription changes. A rising axial-length curve is the earliest warning you can get.
  • It is objective. A chart reading depends on the child's cooperation, the room, even the time of day. A millimeter is a millimeter — same device, same result, comparable across visits.
  • It measures risk. The danger of myopia is not the glasses — it is that an over-stretched eye carries higher lifetime risk of retinal detachment, myopic macular degeneration and glaucoma. Those risks track with axial length, not with the diopter number.

How to use it

1

Get a baseline

Ask for an axial-length measurement at the next eye exam — any optometry or ophthalmology clinic with a biometer can do it. Record both eyes, the date, and the device name.

2

Re-measure on a schedule

Every 3–6 months, at the same clinic and the same device (devices differ slightly; consistency beats precision). One measurement is a dot. Two are a line. Three are a trend.

3

Judge the speed, not the spot

Some elongation is normal childhood growth — roughly 0.1–0.2 mm per year in early school years, slowing to almost zero by the mid-teens. What matters is whether your child's curve is bending faster than that. See the age-by-age reference numbers.

One number. Every 90 days. That is the whole habit.
