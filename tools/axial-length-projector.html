Where does your child's lane end at 18?

One measurement per eye is enough to start. Enter your child's age and the latest axial length for each eye (from any biometry report), pick the reference population that matches your child — the CREAM consortium's European/Australian database (sex-specific curves, ages 6–18) or the Chinese reference (sex-combined, ages 3–18) — and we pinpoint each eye's exact percentile lane, project it to age 18 (axial length and estimated refraction), and grade the risk. Estimated refraction at 18 — from axial length, rule of thumb

Reference data, two options: (1) sex-specific axial-length percentiles for European/Australian children aged 6–21, from the CREAM (Consortium for Refractive Error and Myopia) population database; (2) sex-combined percentile curves for Chinese children aged 3–18. Chinese eyes run longer on average — pick the population your child belongs to for the most meaningful lane. Method note: the projection assumes the eye holds its current percentile lane to age 18 — a simplification used for orientation, not a clinical prediction. The refraction estimate applies the rule of thumb of −2.5 to −3.0 D per mm beyond a 24 mm emmetropic baseline; actual refraction depends on corneal and lens power, which axial length alone cannot see; growth spurts and interventions also move the outcome. Axial length around 26 mm and beyond is the range where high-myopia complications climb steeply. Educational tool, not medical advice.

How it works

1

Enter the measurement

Age plus the latest axial length from any biometry report — enter each eye you have (left required, right optional). Pick the reference population that matches your child: CREAM European/Australian curves (sex-specific, ages 6–18) or the Chinese reference (ages 3–18).

2

Find the lane

The tool anchors the eye on the P5–P95 percentile curves. The lane matters more than the number: a child at a "normal" value who is climbing lanes is still accelerating toward myopia — and that climb is visible years before blur.

3

Project, grade, share

The lane extends to age 18, with an estimated refraction and a four-level risk grade (Low / Moderate / Elevated / High). Download the chart as a PNG or copy the share text — bring it to your next appointment, or post it to compare notes with other parents.

What the lanes tell you

Children's eyes grow along percentile lanes. P50 is the median child; lanes above P75 at a young age mean the eye is longer than three-quarters of peers — and length is the thing that drives myopia. The earlier you know the lane, the cheaper the intervention: light, outdoor time, optical defocus and disciplined tracking beat late, aggressive corrections every time. For the growth-rate numbers behind this tool, see average axial growth rate by age; for what counts as normal at the most-asked age, normal axial length at age 7.

Educational tool, not medical advice. The projection assumes the eye holds its current lane; the refraction estimate uses the −2.5 to −3.0 D per mm rule of thumb beyond a 24 mm baseline. Real eyes also depend on corneal and lens power — interpret results with your eye-care professional.
