Axial Length Percentile Projector

One measurement is enough to start. See exactly which percentile lane your child's eyes are on, where that lane lands at age 18 — axial length and estimated refraction — and how urgent the situation is.

Where does your child's lane end at 18?

One measurement is enough to start. Enter your child's age and latest axial length, pick the reference population that matches your child — the CREAM consortium's European/Australian database (sex-specific curves, ages 6–18) or the Chinese reference (sex-combined, ages 3–18) — and we pinpoint their exact percentile lane, project it to age 18 (axial length and estimated refraction), and grade the risk. Children who hold their lane tend to land where the lane ends; children who climb lanes are the ones to act on early.

CREAM curves are sex-specific.

From the latest biometry report — either eye.

Reference data, two options: (1) sex-specific axial-length percentiles for European/Australian children aged 6–21, from the CREAM (Consortium for Refractive Error and Myopia) population database; (2) sex-combined percentile curves for Chinese children aged 3–18. Chinese eyes run longer on average — pick the population your child belongs to for the most meaningful lane. Method note: the projection assumes the eye holds its current percentile lane to age 18 — a simplification used for orientation, not a clinical prediction. The refraction estimate applies the rule of thumb of −2.5 to −3.0 D per mm beyond a 24 mm emmetropic baseline; actual refraction depends on corneal and lens power, which axial length alone cannot see; growth spurts and interventions also move the outcome. Axial length around 26 mm and beyond is the range where high-myopia complications climb steeply. Educational tool, not medical advice.

How it works

1

Enter one measurement

Age plus the latest axial length from any biometry report — either eye. Pick the reference population that matches your child: CREAM European/Australian curves (sex-specific, ages 6–18) or the Chinese reference (ages 3–18).

2

Find the lane

The tool anchors the eye on the P5–P95 percentile curves. The lane matters more than the number: a child at a "normal" value who is climbing lanes is still accelerating toward myopia — and that climb is visible years before blur.

3

Project, grade, share

The lane extends to age 18, with an estimated refraction and a four-level risk grade (Low / Moderate / Elevated / High). Download the chart as a PNG or copy the share text — bring it to your next appointment, or post it to compare notes with other parents.

What the lanes tell you

Children's eyes grow along percentile lanes. P50 is the median child; lanes above P75 at a young age mean the eye is longer than three-quarters of peers — and length is the thing that drives myopia. The earlier you know the lane, the cheaper the intervention: light, outdoor time, optical defocus and disciplined tracking beat late, aggressive corrections every time.
